A composite index turns many tract metrics into one score that can be mapped, compared and tracked. It is only as trustworthy as the choices behind it, so the three indices here share one construction and differ only in what they measure:

- **Urban Spatial Exclusion Index (USEI).** Being cut off from jobs, services and opportunity by where one lives.
- **Green Index.** Environmental quality and access to green space.
- **Prosperity Index.** Economic vitality. Its academic version reached its 15th iteration, each tightening the method, the validation or the presentation.

> The indices and their inputs are confidential under NDA. The figures on this page build an exclusion index the same way from public American Community Survey data for New York City. It illustrates the method; it is not the USEI.

## One scaffolding

Every index makes the same choices explicit: which metrics enter, how each is scaled so no unit dominates, how the parts are weighted, and how the result is checked. Because the scaffolding is shared, scores are comparable across indices, and an improvement to the method reaches all three at once instead of being re-implemented. Every score is mapped tract by tract, so a high value can always be traced back to the components that drove it.

The Bronx scores highest at 48 and Manhattan lowest at 25. The share without a college degree and rent burden are the two highest-scoring components in every borough: the Bronx averages 81 and 56 on them. Crowding and internet access separate the boroughs far less, from 15 to 32 and from 18 to 29.

A composite index has to survive its own assumptions. The last check rebuilds the index under other reasonable choices and compares the ranking of tracts with the baseline.

The ranking holds. How components are scaled or weighted barely matters, with correlations of 0.985 to 0.999. Dropping a whole dimension matters most, and the correlation still stays between 0.934 and 0.949.
